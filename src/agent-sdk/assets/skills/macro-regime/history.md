# Macro history: regimes and what they did to which stocks

Read this in step 4 of `SKILL.md`, after the analog engine names its nearest months.
Each era lists the setting, what won, what lost, the tell that preceded the turn, and the
lesson that still applies. Figures are approximate and rounded. They are the
remembered record, fine for orientation. Before quoting one in a published read, check it
against a source and cite that source.

The single idea running through all of it: **equities are long-duration claims on nominal
cash flows.** Their price is roughly `cash flow / (real rate + risk premium - growth)`.
A regime is a set of forces acting on those three terms. Growth up helps; real rates up
hurts, and hurts most where the cash flows sit furthest out; the risk premium moves with
credit, volatility and liquidity. Inflation matters through all three at once. Moderate
inflation that lifts nominal growth is fine. Inflation that raises rates faster than
growth, or that makes stocks and bonds fall together, is the enemy.

## 1. Post-war boom, 1946 to 1965

**Setting.** Financial repression. The Fed pegged Treasury yields until the 1951 Accord,
so real rates were negative through the late-1940s inflation burst (CPI about 14% in
1947). Bretton Woods fixed currencies to a gold-linked dollar. The world rebuilt, the baby
boom formed households, the 1956 Interstate Highway Act and suburbanisation drove a decade
of physical capex. Long yields drifted up from about 2% (1946), the start of a 35-year
bond bear market.

**Won.** Industrials, autos (General Motors was the largest US company), steel,
chemicals, oil majors, building materials, homebuilding, consumer durables, utilities,
then in the 1960s the electronics and office-machine growers (IBM, Xerox, Polaroid,
Texas Instruments). The S&P 500 P/E expanded from about 7 in 1949 to about 22 by 1961.

**Lost.** Bondholders in real terms. Cash.

**Lesson.** Negative real rates plus a physical build-out plus a demographic tailwind is
the best equity backdrop there is: earnings grow *and* multiples expand. The same mix,
reconstruction capex with repressed real rates, is what bulls mean when they say "this is
like the 1950s" about an AI or re-industrialisation build-out. Check the real rate before
accepting the analogy. It only holds while real rates stay low.

## 2. Go-go years and the Nifty Fifty, 1966 to 1972

**Setting.** Guns and butter: Vietnam plus the Great Society, with the Fed accommodating.
Inflation rose from about 2% to about 6% by 1970. The 1966 credit crunch, the 1969-70
recession and the 1970 Penn Central collapse (a commercial-paper panic) were warnings.
August 1971: the Nixon shock closed the gold window and imposed wage-price controls.
The Dow first touched 1,000 in 1966 and did not durably clear it until 1982.

**Won, then lost.** The "one-decision" Nifty Fifty (Polaroid, Xerox, Avon, Coca-Cola,
IBM, Disney, McDonald's) reached 40 to 90 times earnings by 1972.

**Lesson.** Quality growth bought at any price is still a duration bet. When inflation and
rates turned in 1973, the Nifty Fifty fell 60 to 90% (Polaroid about -90%, Avon about
-86%). Many were good businesses that took a decade or more to regain their prices. **A
great business at a price that assumes permanent low rates is a rates trade.**

## 3. Oil embargo and the 1973-74 bear market

**Setting.** October 1973 Yom Kippur war; the OAPEC embargo quadrupled oil from about $3
to about $12. CPI reached about 11-12% in 1974; fed funds about 13% in mid-1974. Recession
from late 1973 to early 1975. Bretton Woods was fully gone and the dollar floated.

**Market.** S&P 500 fell about 48% from January 1973 to October 1974, more after
inflation. Stocks and bonds fell together.

**Won.** Oil producers, energy services, coal, gold (from $35 official to about $180 by
end-1974), commodities, farmland.
**Lost.** Nifty Fifty, airlines, autos, consumer discretionary, utilities (Con Edison
omitted its dividend in 1974), REITs (the 1973-75 REIT crash), banks (Franklin National
failed in 1974).

**Tell.** Inflation already rising, an accommodative Fed, stretched growth multiples, and
then an oil supply shock. **An oil shock hits hardest when inflation is already above
target, because the central bank cannot look through it.**

## 4. Stagflation, 1975 to 1979

**Setting.** Burns's Fed kept real rates negative; the dollar sagged; the 1979 Iranian
revolution caused the second oil shock (about $14 to $35-40 by 1980). Gold reached $850 in
January 1980. CPI hit about 13% in 1979. BusinessWeek's "The Death of Equities" cover
(August 1979) was the famous contrarian bottom signal three years early.

**Won.** Energy (about 28-30% of the S&P 500 by 1980), oil services, gold miners,
small-cap value (its best decade), real estate and farmland, commodity producers, Japan.
**Lost.** Long bonds ("certificates of confiscation"), large-cap growth, anything with
fixed nominal cash flows and no pricing power.

**Lesson.** In true stagflation the winners own the scarce real thing (energy, metals,
land) or have pricing power and short-duration cash flows. The quadrant is identified by
growth falling while inflation rises; the stock-bond correlation turns positive; the
dollar weakens. **Positive stock-bond correlation is the fingerprint of an inflation
regime.** `R_STOCK_BOND_CORR` watches for it.

## 5. The Volcker shock, October 1979 to August 1982

**Setting.** The October 6, 1979 "Saturday night special" moved the Fed to targeting
reserves. Fed funds reached about 20% (1981); the prime rate 21.5%; the 10-year peaked
near 15.8% (September 1981), the 30-year near 15.2%. Double-dip recession (1980 and
1981-82); unemployment 10.8% at end-1982; the Latin American debt crisis began in August
1982.

**Market.** The S&P 500 bottomed on August 12, 1982 near 102, at a P/E of about 7-8.
**Lost during.** Housing (mortgage rates about 18%), savings and loans (duration
mismatch), autos, farmland (the 1980s farm crisis), and from 1981 energy (oil fell to
about $10 by 1986).

**Lesson.** Breaking entrenched inflation costs a recession, but **the peak in long
yields was the generational entry point for both bonds and equities.** When a central bank
is visibly willing to cause a recession to anchor inflation, the long end eventually
rallies first. Equities follow once earnings stop falling.

## 6. Disinflationary boom, 1982 to 1989

**Setting.** The 10-year fell from about 14% to about 8%. Reagan tax cuts and deficits; a
strong dollar until the September 1985 Plaza Accord; oil collapsed in 1986; the LBO and
junk-bond era.

**October 19, 1987.** The S&P 500 fell 20.5% in a day (the Dow 22.6%). It came after
the 10-year rose from about 7% to about 10% during 1987, a falling dollar, and portfolio
insurance selling into the decline. **There was no recession.** The market finished 1987
slightly up.

**Won.** Financials, consumer brands, pharma, media, conglomerates being bought out, Japan
(the Nikkei bubble to December 1989). **Lost.** Energy after 1986, farm economy, Texas and
Oklahoma banks.

**Lesson.** Falling rates plus falling inflation is the longest-lasting equity tailwind.
A crash driven by rates and valuation is not the same as a recession; **separate
"multiple shock" drawdowns (fast, recoverable) from "earnings shock" drawdowns (slow,
deeper).**

## 7. Gulf War, S&L crisis and recession, 1990 to 1991

**Setting.** Iraq invaded Kuwait in August 1990 and oil doubled (about $17 to about $40
by October). Recession from July 1990 to March 1991. More than 1,000 savings and loans
failed (resolved by the RTC); commercial real estate collapsed; banks rationed credit.
The Fed cut from about 8% to 3% by 1992.

**Market.** S&P 500 fell about 20% from July to October 1990, then rallied as oil fell
back when the war began.

**Won after.** Banks. A steep curve rebuilt their capital (buy deposits near 3%, lend or
own Treasuries at 7%). Then technology.

**Lesson.** An oil spike plus a credit crunch is a recession recipe. **The recovery trade
is a steep curve: banks and small caps.** Oil spikes driven by war tend to reverse once
the uncertainty resolves. Energy equities are a hedge while the event is live, not a
long-term hold on the spike.

## 8. The 1994 bond massacre

**Setting.** The Fed raised rates from 3% to 6% between February 1994 and February 1995
into a strengthening economy. The 10-year rose from about 5.2% to about 8%. **MBS
convexity amplified it.** As rates rose, prepayments slowed, mortgage duration extended,
and hedgers sold Treasuries to rebalance, pushing rates higher still. Orange County went
bankrupt (December 1994), Mexico devalued (the tequila crisis), and several levered
mortgage funds blew up (Askin).

**Market.** S&P 500 about -1.5% on price in 1994, a choppy range. Then +34% in 1995 once
the Fed stopped.

**Won.** Short-duration cash-flow businesses, cyclicals tied to the strong economy,
technology into 1995. **Lost.** Long bonds, utilities, mortgage REITs, emerging markets,
anything levered to rates.

**Lesson, and the closest analog to a Fed hiking into strength with a long-end rout.**
With no recession, a hiking cycle into a strong economy tends to produce a sideways,
choppy year in stocks, then a strong year once the Fed signals a stop. **The MOVE index
and the mortgage spread are the tell for convexity-driven overshoots.** The top in long
yields usually comes at the end of the hiking cycle or slightly before it.

## 9. Productivity boom and dot-com mania, 1995 to March 2000

**Setting.** Real productivity gains, falling inflation, a balanced budget, the internet.
Greenspan's "irrational exuberance" speech (December 1996) came more than three years
early. The 1997 Asian crisis, then the August-September 1998 Russia default and LTCM
collapse (S&P about -19%, three Fed cuts). Y2K liquidity was added in late 1999. The Fed
then hiked from 4.75% to 6.5% (June 1999 to May 2000), and oil tripled from about $11
(December 1998) to above $30.

**Market.** Nasdaq rose from 1,000 (1995) to 5,048 (March 10, 2000). The S&P 500
cyclically adjusted P/E reached about 44, the highest on record.

**Won.** Tech, telecom, media, internet (Cisco, Microsoft, Intel, Oracle, Sun; Qualcomm
rose about 2,600% in 1999). **Lost.** Value, old economy, small value, REITs; Buffett was
said to have lost his touch.

**Lesson.** A real technology revolution and a financial bubble can coexist. The telecom
build-out was debt-financed and over-built; **capex financed by debt at rising rates is
the tell that the cycle is maturing.** Today's echo is hyperscaler and AI-infrastructure
borrowing competing with the Treasury for long-dated capital.

## 10. Dot-com bust, 2000 to 2002

**Setting.** Recession from March to November 2001; 9/11; the Enron (December 2001) and
WorldCom (July 2002) frauds. The Fed cut from 6.5% to 1.75% in 2001, and to 1% by 2003.

**Market.** Nasdaq -78% peak to trough (October 2002); S&P 500 -49%.

**Won.** Value, small caps, REITs, homebuilders (falling rates), energy, gold (bottomed
near $255 in 2001), Treasuries. **Lost.** Tech, telecom equipment (Lucent and Nortel about
-99%), media.

**Lesson.** **Rate cuts do not rescue bubble equities while their earnings are
collapsing.** Leadership changes after a bubble: value, small caps and real assets
outperformed large growth for six years. The "Fed put" works for multiples, not for broken
earnings.

## 11. Credit and housing boom, 2003 to 2006

**Setting.** Fed at 1% until mid-2004, then 17 "measured pace" hikes to 5.25% by June
2006. Greenspan's "conundrum": long rates barely rose, held down by foreign reserve
buying. China's super-cycle took oil from about $30 to about $78 (2006), on the way to
$147 in July 2008. Subprime, securitisation and CDOs.

**Won.** Energy, materials, industrials, emerging markets, homebuilders (to a 2005 peak),
banks and brokers, REITs. **Lost.** Relatively, large-cap tech. Absolute returns were
fine.

**Lesson.** **Gradual, telegraphed hikes with a calm long end are compatible with a bull
market.** The damage built up quietly in credit and housing, not in the equity index.
Homebuilders peaked in mid-2005, more than two years before the S&P.

## 12. Global financial crisis, 2007 to 2009

**Setting.** Home prices peaked in 2006. Subprime cracked in February 2007 (HSBC, New
Century). August 2007: BNP froze funds and quant funds had their "quake". Bear Stearns
failed in March 2008; oil hit $147 in July; Lehman failed on September 15, 2008, followed
by AIG and TARP. The Fed went to zero in December 2008 and started QE1.

**Market.** S&P 500 -57% (October 2007 to March 9, 2009, low 676). HY OAS about 20%.
VIX closed near 80 in November 2008. Unemployment reached 10% (October 2009).

**Won.** Treasuries, the dollar, gold (after late 2008), relatively staples and health
care. **Lost.** Financials (Citigroup about -98%), homebuilders, REITs, consumer
discretionary, small caps, emerging markets, commodities (oil to about $32).

**Tells, in order.** The curve inverted in 2006-07; permits and homebuilders rolled over
from 2005-06; bank stocks peaked in February 2007; **credit spreads widened from mid-2007
while the S&P 500 made a new high in October 2007.** Credit leads equities at the top.

**Lesson.** Housing plus leverage plus MBS is systemic because the collateral is
everywhere. Watch the weak tail (CCC spreads, `C_CCC_GAP`), bank relative strength
(`E_BANKS`), home prices (`H_HOME_PRICES`) and SLOOS tightening (`C_SLOOS`).

## 13. QE and ZIRP, 2009 to 2015

**Setting.** QE1, QE2, Operation Twist, QE3. The euro crisis (2010-12) ended with
Draghi's "whatever it takes" (July 2012). The US was downgraded in August 2011 (S&P
-19%). **The taper tantrum** (May to June 2013): the 10-year went from about 1.6% to about
3.0% on a hint of slower purchases; emerging markets, REITs and utilities fell; MBS
convexity again. Oil collapsed from about $107 (2014) to about $26 (February 2016), with
a wave of energy high-yield defaults. China devalued in August 2015.

**Won.** US large-cap growth (the FAANG era), biotech (to 2015), bond proxies (utilities,
staples, REITs) under "there is no alternative", buybacks, private equity. **Lost.** Banks
(flat curve, thin margins), energy after 2014, emerging markets, commodities, European and
Japanese banks. Value against growth had its longest drawdown on record.

**Lesson.** **Negative real yields plus abundant reserves reward duration:** long-duration
growth and bond proxies. A hint that liquidity is being withdrawn (the 2013 tantrum) hits
the same assets first.

## 14. Late cycle, QT and the repo spike, 2016 to 2019

**Setting.** Hikes from December 2015 to 2.25-2.5% by December 2018. QT on "autopilot"
from 2017. The 2017 tax cut. "Volmageddon" in February 2018 (short-vol products
collapsed). In Q4 2018 the S&P 500 fell 19.8% after Powell said on October 3 that the Fed
was "a long way from neutral", with the 10-year at 3.23% and QT running. Powell pivoted in
January 2019; there were three cuts in 2019. On September 17, 2019 repo spiked (SOFR
about 5.25%) as reserves became scarce, and the Fed restarted bill purchases.

**Lesson.** **The pain threshold for yields depends on the era: level and speed relative
to recent history, not an absolute number.** 3.2% broke things in 2018 because it was
the high of the decade and came with QT. Funding markets (`L_FUNDING`) are the tell that
balance-sheet runoff has gone too far.

## 15. COVID and money printing, 2020 to 2021

**Setting.** The S&P 500 fell 34% in 23 trading days in February-March 2020; VIX closed
at 82.69 on March 16, 2020. The Fed went to zero with unlimited QE and corporate-credit
facilities. Fiscal packages totalled about $5 trillion (CARES, December 2020, ARP). M2
grew about 27% y/y at its peak (February 2021), the fastest since WWII. WTI futures
settled at -$37.63 on April 20, 2020. Mortgage rates fell to about 2.65% (January 2021);
home prices rose about 20% y/y.

**Won.** In 2020: stay-at-home tech, e-commerce, software, Tesla, ARKK (about +150% in
2020). In 2021: cyclicals, energy, banks, housing, meme stocks (GameStop, January 2021),
SPACs, crypto (Bitcoin near $69,000 in November 2021). **Lost.** In 2020: energy, travel,
office REITs, banks.

**Lesson.** **Money printing inflates everything with duration and speculation, in the
order of risk.** The reverse ran in the same order in 2022: crypto and unprofitable tech
peaked first (February to November 2021), then the index. `L_NET_LIQ`, `L_M2` and the
`crypto_high_beta` archetype watch this channel.

## 16. The inflation shock and the fastest hiking cycle, 2022 to 2023

**Setting.** CPI peaked at 9.1% (June 2022), the highest since 1981. The Fed went from
0-0.25% to 5.25-5.5% by July 2023 (four 75bp hikes), plus QT. Russia invaded Ukraine on
February 24, 2022; Brent hit about $128 and European gas spiked. The 10-year rose from
1.5% to 4.25% (October 2022), then to 4.99% (October 19, 2023); the 30-year mortgage
reached 7.79% (October 2023). The UK LDI crisis came in September 2022. SVB failed on
March 10, 2023 on duration losses in its held-to-maturity book, followed by Signature
and First Republic; the Fed created the BTFP.

**Market.** S&P 500 -25% (January to October 2022); Nasdaq -33% in 2022; a 60/40
portfolio had its worst year in about a century; TLT fell about 31% in 2022; ARKK fell
about 80% peak to trough; DXY reached about 114; stocks and bonds fell together.

**Won.** Energy (XLE about +65% in 2022), value, defensives, the dollar, T-bills,
commodities. **Lost.** Long-duration growth, unprofitable tech, crypto (FTX, November
2022), REITs, long bonds, regional banks (2023).

**The 2023 term-premium episode.** From August to October 2023 the 10-year rose about
100bp on larger coupon auctions, the Fitch downgrade (August 1, 2023) and a rising term
premium. It reversed on November 1, 2023, when the refunding statement slowed coupon
increases and the Fed paused. Stocks and bonds rallied hard into year-end. Meanwhile
ChatGPT (November 30, 2022) started the AI trade; the largest seven stocks roughly doubled
in 2023 while breadth stayed narrow.

**Lessons.** (1) **In an inflation regime, bonds do not hedge stocks.** Hold cash,
T-bills, commodities, or buy options. (2) **Duration mismatches break banks** (SVB).
Watch `E_BANKS` and unrealised losses when the long end sells off. (3) **Term-premium
spikes can end on a refunding statement.** The quarterly refunding date belongs on every
events list.

## 17. AI capex, higher for longer, and re-tightening, 2024 to 2026

The data files and the dated news record this era better than memory does. Cite them.

- **2024.** The Fed cut 100bp (September to December) while the 10-year *rose* from about
  3.6% to about 4.8% by January 2025: a bear steepening on cuts, driven by the term
  premium and fiscal worries. On August 5, 2024 the yen-carry unwind took the VIX to about
  65 intraday and the Nikkei down 12% in a day (`L_YEN_CARRY`).
- **2025.** "Liberation Day" tariffs (April 2, 2025) drove the S&P 500 down about 19% from
  its February peak. Unusually, the 10-year *rose* and the dollar *fell* at the same time
  ("sell America"), until the 90-day pause on April 9. Gold passed $3,000. A record-long
  government shutdown in October-November 2025 left gaps in the data: October 2025 CPI
  was never published, which is why the engine counts calendar months, not rows.
- **2026, per this skill's dated news runs.** Read the latest
  `data/research/*/macro/news.json`. The September 2026 run recorded: Kevin Warsh as Fed
  chair; a 25bp hike on 2026-09-16 to 3.75-4.00% (the first since 2023); a US-Iran conflict
  with Hormuz disruption and Brent above $100; the 10-year above 5% (highest since 2007)
  and the 30-year near 5.45% (highest since 2004); hyperscaler debt issuance described as
  "competition for capital"; mortgage rates above 7%; HY spreads still near 270bp; and a
  Q3 S&P 500 EPS growth estimate near 29%.

**Lesson in progress.** An earnings boom (AI capex) running into a rising discount rate:
it echoes 1994 (hikes into strength with convexity), 1999 (capex boom, Fed hiking, oil up)
and 2023 (a term-premium spike). Which one it becomes depends on whether earnings keep
outrunning the discount rate, and on what breaks first if they do not.

## Cross-era rules

| Pattern | Seen in | What it did to stocks |
|---|---|---|
| Oil +50% or more within a year with inflation already above target | 1973, 1979, 1990, 2008, 2022 | Recession or bear market followed in four of five; energy the only safe equity sector |
| Fed hiking into strong growth, no credit stress | 1994, 2004-06, 2017-18 | Choppy, flat-to-modest index; rotation to short-duration cash flows; the rally comes when hikes stop |
| Curve re-steepening from deep inversion | 1990, 2001, 2007, 2019-20 | Recession typically began within 0-12 months of the re-steepening |
| Credit widening while equities make highs | 1998, 2007, 2015, 2018 | Equities followed credit down within months |
| Positive stock-bond correlation | 1966-1997, 2022-2023 | Inflation regime; 60/40 fails; real assets and cash hedge better than bonds |
| Real 10-year above about 2.5% | 2007, 2023, 2025-26 | Long-duration growth de-rates unless earnings growth is exceptional |
| Mortgage rate above 7% with MOVE above 100 | 2000, 2022-23 | Existing-home turnover freezes; builders survive on buydowns at thinner margins |
| M2 or Fed balance sheet surge | 2009-13, 2020-21 | Speculative assets lead, in order of risk; reversal runs in the same order |
| Bubble peak valuations meet rising rates | 1972, 2000, 2021 | Former leaders fall 70-90%; leadership changes for years |
| Peak in long yields with the central bank visibly hawkish | 1981, 1994, 2023 | The generational or cyclical entry point for duration, then equities |
